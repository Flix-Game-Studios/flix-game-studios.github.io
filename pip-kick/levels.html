Pip Kick · Retention memo

An endless game has no destination. That is the whole problem.

The mechanic is good and the systems around it are done. What is missing is a reason to open the app on Tuesday. Below: why levels are the answer, how to build them into this codebase specifically, and the four other things worth doing once they exist.

After 97 checks and a working iOS build Endless, daily, pass · 3 characters · 7 hats

The honest diagnosis

Pip Kick has a lot of systems and no destination. Those are different problems and only one of them is solved.

Right now the game has chains, a pot you can bank, tricks, chapters, missions, unlocks, hats, a daily, a pace ghost and a continue. That is a genuinely rich set of mechanics. But every one of them is a way of scoring, and the run always ends the same way — you drop it, and the only thing that changed is a number.

There is never a moment where the game says you did the thing. No completion, no closure, nothing finished. Which means there is nothing to come back to — only something to come back at.

The number that should decide this

A pure endless arcade game retains roughly 30% at day one and 5% at day seven. That is the genre, not a failure of execution. Level-based casual games in the same install bracket routinely hold 45% and 15% — three times the week-one survival, from the same core loop, because a level ends and an endless run does not.

Why "one more level" beats "one more run"

  • A level has an ending you can see coming. "One more run" is an open-ended commitment; "one more level" is a bounded one, and people say yes to bounded things at bedtime.
  • Failing a level tells you something. "You needed five perfects, you got three" is actionable. "You scored 80" is not. Actionable failure is what makes a retry feel like progress instead of repetition.
  • Progress becomes visible and cumulative. 47 of 120 with gold on 31 of them is a commitment device. A personal best is a single number that erases its own history every time it moves.
  • Designed content beats a difficulty curve. Endless can only get harder. Levels can get different — and difference is what stops the twentieth session feeling like the second.

How levels work here

The mechanic is already a skill test, which is exactly the thing level design is good at framing. A level is one objective plus at most two modifiers, and almost all of the parts exist already.

Objectives — each one is a different game

This is where variety comes from. The same tap, framed twelve ways:

Every one of these already exists as a field on the run summary. The objective system is a predicate over an object the game is already building — not new simulation, just new reading of it.

Three stars, because one pass mark wastes the level

A level you cleared once is finished forever. A level you cleared with one star is an open loop you will come back to. Same content, several times the lifespan — this is the single highest-leverage decision in the whole system.

  • One star — meet the objective. Generous; nobody should be stuck.
  • Two stars — a good run by the player the world is written for.
  • Three stars — a good run by someone better than that.

These were originally a blanket ×1.35 and ×1.8 on every level regardless of type, which could never suit both Score 40 and Reach a chain of 6. They are now measured — see the budget below.

Every attempt has a kick budget

This is the thing that makes a level a level, and it was missing. A run only ended when you dropped it, and a patient player never drops it: kick in the GOOD band forever and nothing in the rules stops you. So every threshold was either free — keep going until you pass it — or, for the objectives that checked the kick count at the end, impossible. Score 90 in 12 kicks asked you to score 90 and then stop playing; carrying on after you had earned it failed the level.

An attempt now lasts a fixed number of kicks and then ends on its own terms. Not a drop — the pot is paid into the score rather than lost, because you did not fail, you finished. Roughly ten to twenty-four kicks in world one, up to about seventy later; survival objectives are the exception, since for them the kick count is the objective.

The rally gets faster

The endless game changed how it looked every chapter and how one rule worked, but never how fast it went, so a long run was a test of patience rather than of anything getting harder. Playtesting on a phone put it plainly: does the ball ever drop faster? It did not.

The descent now accelerates with the kick count — flat for the first twelve kicks, then climbing to +35% by kick 67 and holding there. Only the fall is scaled. Scaling the rise as well would lower the apex and quietly change which scoring band every kick lands in, which is a different game rather than a faster one. The window to read a PERFECT drops from 344ms to 255ms; median rallies shorten by roughly a third, and a run now ends because it got quick rather than because attention wandered.

It is deterministic in the kick count, so the daily challenge stays reproducible. Every level number below was re-derived underneath it.

The numbers were played before they shipped

tools/tune-levels.mjs drives the real simulation with a bot at four skill levels and reports what each level actually yields. Its model of skill is a target height to wait for and a reaction time — aim for the bottom of the window and a late tap misses entirely, which is the game's real tension rather than an invented one. A calibrated lapse rate bounds the rally, because otherwise a patient bot plays forever.

It is a very good detector: it found the unbounded run above, found that passes was missing from the finished summary so every rally level graded as zero however well it went, and found eight levels that could not be beaten at any skill. It is a poor designer — it swipes a trick on most kicks and never gets bored, and letting it write the ladder produced “reach chapter 1” as the final boss. So it sets the budgets, caps three stars where it can prove the target unreachable, and the asks stay hand-written.

The first answer blamed the levels

Ten levels came back “brutal” — a 20–35% clear rate for the strongest bot — and they clustered on the heavy and double modifiers. That looked like a difficulty finding. It was not. The bot waited for the shuttlecock to reach a target height and then tapped after its reaction delay, so every contact landed vy × delay lower than it intended; under a faster fall it drove itself into the ground. It read “falls faster” as “unplayable” because it had never learned to go early.

Players lead the ball. Giving the bot the same anticipation — a skill-dependent fraction of the delay, from 0.35 for a novice to 0.95 for an expert — took the flagged count from ten to two without touching a single level. The eleven asks that had already been lowered on that bad evidence were put back.

What survived was one real economy finding: banking pays about 60 points over a world-one attempt and about 120 over a world-three one, so Bank 200 points was never possible. Those two asks came down, and banking levels now get the longer attempt their chain-building needs.

Before: 27 of 60 flagged, 8 unbeatable, 2 free. After: 0 flagged over three independent runs — every level beatable, none trivial, and no level where clearing hands you all three stars. Three stars sits between 1.4× and 2.0× one star across the whole ladder. The clear rate falls from 57% to 37% for a middling player between world one and world five, which is the curve.

Stars are the currency. They gate worlds, hats and characters — replacing the mission stars that currently do that job, which keeps one economy instead of two.

The pattern to keep

Levels 1–5 introduce one idea each and nothing else. From 6, the shape is hard, easy, easy, hard with a boss every fifth and a new world every tenth. Characters unlock at bosses, not at score thresholds — an unlock that arrives as a reward for a named achievement is worth several times one that arrives because a number crossed a line.

Building it into this codebase

Most of the work is already done. The simulation, the modifiers, the run summary and the star economy all exist — this is mostly assembly. 

src/levels.js      level table + objective predicates + star thresholds
src/game.js        g.level, objective progress on each kick, pass/fail at finish
src/levelmap.js    the map screen: nodes, stars, locked worlds
src/save.js        v8: per-level stars, furthest unlocked
src/ui.js          objective banner during play, results card after

A level is data, not code:

{ id: 5, world: 1, boss: false,
  goal: { type: "scoreIn", n: 90, kicks: 12 },
  mods: [],
  stars: [90, 122, 162],            // one, two, three
  budget: 12,                       // kicks in the attempt
  hint: "Safe kicks will not get you there." }

An objective is a predicate over the summary the game already builds: 

scoreIn: (s, g) => s.kicks <= g.kicks && s.score >= g.score,
noSafe:  (s, g) => s.kicks >= g.kicks && !s.bands.includes("S"),
chain:   (s, g) => s.bestChain >= g.chain,
Objectives and the results card
~6h

The level table, the predicates, star thresholds, and a result screen that says what you needed and what you got. Playable against a hardcoded list of ten before any map exists.

Objective banner and live progress
~3h

The goal sits in the HUD during the run — "4 / 6 perfects" — so you always know whether this attempt is still alive. A level whose failure you only learn about at the end is a level you will not retry.

The map
~8h

Nodes down a scrolling path, stars under each, worlds as coloured bands using the chapter palettes. Mark levels you failed narrowly — "you were one perfect away" — because that is the near-miss at map scale, and it is the strongest pull on the screen.

The four new modifiers
~4h

Blind, heavy, locked, sudden. Blind first — hiding the ground glow is one line and changes the game more than the other three together.

Design and tune 60 levels
~14h

The actual craft. The first twenty by hand from the table above; the rest generated from objective × modifier combinations and then played and adjusted. Star thresholds want a pass over real runs, not guesses.

Fold the economies together
~3h

Level stars replace mission stars. Missions become a daily side-quest that also pays stars, rather than the only source. One currency.

Roughly 38 hours to a 60-level game. Endless stays as an Arcade mode on the menu, and the daily is untouched.

The four other things, ranked

AddWhy it worksEstImpact
Local notifications "Today's daily is up." Needs no account and no server — the phone schedules it. This is the cheapest retention point available anywhere in the product, and it is the only mechanism that reaches someone who has closed the app. 3h Very high
Collections Hats already work. Add trails, shuttlecock feathers, ground patterns and outfits — all procedural, so a new one costs a few paths rather than an art commission. Collecting is the most reliable long-term retention driver in casual games, and here it is nearly free. 10h High
Daily streak Seven consecutive days pays a hat. Slightly cynical, extremely effective, and it pairs with the notification rather than duplicating it. 3h Medium
Weekly level pack Five new levels every Monday. Turns a finite game into a subscription-shaped one without a subscription. Only worth it once the level system exists and people are finishing worlds. ongoing Later

What not to build

  • Energy or lives. It buys a little revenue and costs the entire goodwill of a charm-led game. Wrong instrument for this product, and a level system does not need one to work.
  • Real-time multiplayer. Async comparison gives most of the social value for none of the netcode.
  • A second currency. Stars do everything. Coins on top of stars is a spreadsheet, not a game.
  • Loot boxes. Regulatory exposure, and completely at odds with what these characters are.

The order I would do it

Notifications first, because they are three hours and reach people who have already left. Then levels, because they are the reason to come back to.

  1. Local notifications — 3h. Nothing else you can build this week will move retention as far.
  2. Levels 1–20 with objectives and a results card — 13h. Ship a playable ladder before building the map; if the objectives are not fun, no map will save them.
  3. The map — 8h. Only once the levels are known to work.
  4. Blind, heavy, locked, sudden — 4h, then design out to sixty levels.
  5. Collections — 10h, once there is a star economy worth spending.
The one thing to be honest about

None of this changes the ceiling set by distribution. A game with levels and notifications retains three times better than one without — but three times a very small audience is still a very small audience. The characters remain the asset, and the pose editor and sticker pack that already exist are still the shortest path to people knowing this game is here at all.
