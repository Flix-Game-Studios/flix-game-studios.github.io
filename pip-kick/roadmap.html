Pip Kick · Direction memo

You don’t have a game problem. You have an IP you haven’t spent yet.

The prototype works — the core tap is tense and the scoring rule does its job. What’s missing isn’t polish, it’s a reason to open the app tomorrow. Below: what to build, in what order, and the honest arithmetic on what it pays.

Prototype v0.1 Canvas · single file · no engine github.com/38st/pip-kick

Start from what’s actually true

You came to this with three characters and no mechanic. That ordering matters more than anything else in this document.

Most mobile games start from a mechanic and bolt on a skin. Yours starts from Pip, Zip and Blaze — designs with strong silhouettes, a coherent world, and genuine charm. That’s the rarer half. Mechanics are cheap; characters people actually like are not.

So the strategic question is not “how do we make this game bigger.” It’s “what is the game for.” Two honest answers, and they lead to completely different builds:

Path A
Hyper-casual

Free, ad-saturated, engineered for install volume. Success needs millions of downloads bought with paid user acquisition, then arbitraged against ad revenue.

Hard pass. That arbitrage stopped working years ago, the publishers who survived (Voodoo, Homa, Supersonic) own the funnel and most of the upside, and the model treats your characters as interchangeable. You’d be renting out the one asset you own.

Path B · Recommended
Character-led

The game is the front door to the characters, not the whole business. Ship it free with light monetization, but treat Pip as a property that also lives in short-form animation, stickers, and eventually physical goods.

This is the one. It matches what you actually have, it doesn’t require a UA budget, and its ceiling is many times higher than the game’s own ad revenue — see the arithmetic below.

The consequence

If Path B is right, then every feature gets judged on whether it makes the characters more lovable or more shareable, not just whether it extends session length. That reorders the entire backlog — which is why the graphics work below outranks most of the gameplay work.

Graphics

The renderer is already the right architecture: drawChar() builds each character from a joint skeleton with a two-pass ink/body outline, so poses are parameters rather than sprites. That means animation improvements are code, not art hours. Don’t throw it out for sprite sheets — extend it.

Ranked by what actually changes how the game feels, cheapest first:

ChangeWhy it mattersEffortImpact
Squash & stretch The single biggest gap. Rubber-hose animation is deformation — Pip currently stays rigid through a kick. Compress on contact, overshoot on release, squash the shuttlecock on impact. The pose system already takes parameters; this is one more. ~30 lines Transformative
Line boil Redraw outlines with 1–2px of random jitter at 8fps instead of 60. Instantly reads as hand-inked rather than computed. Almost free, and it’s the difference between “canvas demo” and “cartoon.” Tiny Very high
Anticipation A 3-frame wind-up before the leg swings. Right now the kick is instantaneous, which is why it reads mechanical. Classic principle, and it makes timing legible. Small High
Sideline crowd Put the other two characters at the edges reacting — cheering a chain, wincing at a near-drop. Reuses drawChar() entirely, and it’s the cheapest personality you will ever buy. Also the thing that makes clips shareable. Medium High
Palette chapters Every 25 kicks, rotate the whole background palette (red → ochre → teal → dusk). Gives an endless run visible chapters, makes long runs feel earned, and turns a screenshot into proof of skill. Medium High
Smear frames On a fast kick, stretch the leg along its motion vector for one frame. What sells speed in hand-drawn animation. Subtle but it’s the texture people can’t name and always notice. Medium Medium
Shuttlecock trail A short ribbon showing the arc. Makes the physics readable and the screenshots better. Small Medium
Layered flat background Keep the flat colour fields — they’re your brand — but add parallaxing mid-century poster shapes behind. Resist illustrated scenery; it would fight the graphic style you already have. Medium Medium
Do these two first

Squash & stretch plus line boil is under an hour of work and will do more for how the game feels than every gameplay feature on the next list combined. Feel is not a polish phase — in an arcade game it is the product.

Gameplay

The honest diagnosis: one verb, no depth curve, no reason to return. The tap is good. Everything around it is missing. Fix in this order:

FeatureWhat it solvesEffortImpact
Pass mode A second character on the far side; rally back and forth. This is already drawn in your reference art — Pip passing the shuttlecock to Zip. It activates the horizontal axis, which is currently dead space, and turns a solo test into a relationship. Best single addition on this page. Medium Very high
Daily challenge One fixed seed a day, everyone gets the identical run, one attempt. The highest-retention feature per line of code in the entire genre. Gives people a reason to open the app that isn’t boredom. Small Very high
Run chapters Pair the palette shift with a modifier — gusty, low-gravity, double-or-nothing. Converts a flat endless run into an escalating gauntlet with memorable moments to talk about. Medium High
Swipe = trick kick A second verb. Worth double, but sends the shuttlecock higher and less predictably — risk stacked on the existing risk. Adds a decision to every touch instead of just a timing window. Small High
Bank the chain Cash out your multiplier for guaranteed points, or push and risk it all. Turns a reflex game into a game with a gambling decision — the thing that makes runs feel like yours. Small Medium
Missions “Five perfects in a row.” “Score 100 as Blaze.” Drip-feeds goals and pushes people onto characters they’d otherwise ignore. Also the natural hook for cosmetic rewards later. Medium Medium
Targets & hazards Rings to kick through, birds to dodge. Adds aiming, punishes autopilot. Lower priority — it risks muddying a mechanic whose strength is that it’s one clean idea. Medium Careful

What not to build

  • Levels and stars. Endless arcade with a daily seed is a better fit and a fraction of the content cost.
  • An energy or lives system. It buys a little revenue and costs you the entire goodwill of a charm-led game. Wrong instrument for this product.
  • Multiplayer. Async leaderboards give you most of the social value for none of the netcode. Real-time is a trap at this stage.

Money

Here is the part most design docs soften. I’d rather give you the real numbers, because they change what you should build.

What to actually implement

LeverDetailVerdict
Rewarded “revive” Watch an ad to continue after a drop, once per run. Highest-yield placement in the genre by a wide margin — typically 40–60% of ad revenue — and the only ad players actively want. US eCPM roughly $15–30. Ship it
Remove ads One-time $2.99. Converts 0.5–2% typically; charm-led games land at the top of that band. Cheap to build, no ongoing cost. Ship it
Cosmetic packs Costumes, hats, alternate palettes. Your art style is unusually well-suited — bold silhouettes take accessories cleanly, and the procedural renderer means a hat is a few paths, not a re-drawn sprite sheet. $0.99–$2.99, or $7.99 for the lot. Ship it
Interstitials Between runs. Real revenue, real retention damage. If you use them, cap hard at one per three runs and never on the first session. Sparingly
Battle pass Needs roughly 50k daily actives before the content treadmill pays for itself. Not a v1 conversation. Later

A single pin sold to 500 people earns roughly what 50,000 downloads earn. Mobile games are a distribution business wearing a product costume — and distribution is exactly what a character with an audience gives you. The game is how people meet Pip. It is not, realistically, how Pip pays for himself.

Which means the real work is upstream

Games that launch cold on the App Store die on day one; discovery there is effectively zero without either a UA budget or external traffic. Since Path A is out, you need the traffic.

  • Post the characters before the game exists. Short animated loops of Pip doing small dumb things. Your renderer can already export these — it’s a pose system, so it's an animation tool you happen to have written.
  • Make a sticker pack now. Free, near-zero effort from the art you have, and it puts the characters into people’s messages — which is distribution you can’t buy.
  • Launch the game to an audience that already exists, even a small one. Ten thousand people who like Pip beat a hundred thousand cold installs on every metric that matters.

Ship order

This is a real sequence — each stage makes the next one worth doing.

Make it feel right
A weekend

Squash & stretch, line boil, anticipation frames, shuttlecock trail. No new features. The game should feel twice as good with the same rules — and if it doesn’t, nothing after this will save it.

Give it a second day
A week

Daily challenge with a fixed seed, local leaderboard, swipe trick kick, banking the chain. This is the point at which it stops being a demo.

Bring the cast in
A week or two

Pass mode with Zip, sideline reactions from whoever isn’t playing, palette chapters. The characters stop being skins and start being a cast.

Build the audience
Ongoing, starts now

Sticker pack, short-form animation loops, a landing page. Runs in parallel with everything above — it has the longest lead time, so it cannot start last.

Wrap and ship
Two to three weeks

Capacitor or Expo wrapper, rewarded revive, remove-ads IAP, first cosmetic pack, store listing. Ship to the audience stage four built, not to a cold store.

One more idea worth taking seriously

The renderer you have is an animation tool that thinks it’s a game.

drawChar() takes a pose and draws any of the three characters in the house style, at any scale, instantly. That is the expensive part of a cartoon pipeline, and you already built it by accident. A small editor on top — pose keyframes, a timeline, export to GIF or MP4 — would let you produce short-form character content in minutes instead of days.

For Path B that’s not a side quest, it’s the engine of the whole plan: the thing that makes “post the characters constantly” sustainable for one person. Worth a weekend, well before the game is finished.
